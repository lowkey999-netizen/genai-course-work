Step 1: Text → Token ID (The Vocabulary Lookup)

The computer does not know what letters are. Before the Nomic neural network can touch the word "king", it must be mapped to a clean integer using a pre-compiled lookup dictionary.

What just happened?

The Nomic tokenizer has a fixed vocabulary table containing ~30,522 token IDs. The word "king" matches entry index 3528. At this point, the number 3528 has ZERO semantic meaning. It is just a row number in a giant table—exactly like an ID badge at a factory door!

Step 2: What the Hell is a Vector? (Geometrically & Mathematically)

People throw around the word "vector" like it's magic. Stripped of hype: A vector is just an ordered list of numbers that describes an arrow pointing from the origin (0, 0, 0) to a specific destination point in space.

What the numbers DO represent:
  • A coordinate along a specific direction learned during training.
  • A relative position compared to every other word in the dictionary.
  • A direction and length (an arrow in geometric space).
What the numbers DO NOT represent:
  • They are not ASCII letters or character codes.
  • They are not human-labeled tags (no one named Dimension 42 "Royalty").
  • Individual numbers cannot be interpreted in isolation; only their combined geometry matters.

Step 3: How Nomic Produces the 768-D Vector (The Transformer Pipeline)

How does the number 3528 become a 768-dimensional coordinate list? Here is the exact physical journey inside the neural network:

1. Lookup
The Initial Embedding Matrix Lookup (Row 3528)

The model holds a giant spreadsheet of size 30,522 × 768. Token ID 3528 acts as an index: it simply copies Row #3528 out of the spreadsheet. This gives an initial raw vector of 768 numbers.

2. Attention
Multi-Head Self-Attention Layers (Mixing the Context)

If the input is "the king of England", the word "king" must borrow meaning from "England". The 12 transformer layers calculate Attention Weights: matrices of numbers asking "how much does word A care about word B?". The vector is updated 12 times through matrix multiplication and non-linear activations.

3. Pooling
Mean Pooling (Collapsing into One Vector)

If you fed a 10-word sentence into Nomic, you now have 10 vectors of 768 numbers each. Nomic averages them together element-by-element (Mean Pooling) and normalizes the vector length to 1.0. Result: Exactly one single vector of 768 floats!

What the Real Nomic Vector Actually Looks Like (First 10 of 768):
[-0.0381, 0.0512, -0.0194, 0.0827, -0.0049, 0.0215, -0.0743, 0.0118, 0.0632, -0.0298, ... +758 more numbers]

Notice that the numbers are all small decimals between $-1.0$ and $+1.0$. This is because the vector has been normalized so its length is $1.0$ (a unit vector on a 768-dimensional sphere).

Step 4: What is an Embedding Space? (Geometry & Vector Math)

An Embedding Space is simply a coordinate system where words are positioned such that semantic similarity = geometric proximity (small angle or short distance between arrows).

Step 5: How SVD Powers PCA (The Dimensionality Reduction Engine)

Now we arrive at the core mystery. You have 10 words, each with 768 numbers. Your computer monitor only has 2 axes ($X$ and $Y$). How do we drop 766 dimensions without destroying all the clustering information?

Interactive SVD Step-by-Step

Watch the data get centered, rotated by SVD, and flattened onto the Principal Component axis!

Original data points scattered in space with a clear diagonal tilt.
Step 1: Subtract the Mean (Center the Cloud)

Look at line 94 in tokens_utils.py:
xc = x - x.mean(axis=0)
Before finding the spread of the data, we calculate the average center of all points and slide the whole cloud so its center sits right at (0, 0).

The SVD Formula: $X = U \cdot \Sigma \cdot V^T$
$U$
Word positions along new axes
$\Sigma$ (Sigma)
Importance / spread of each axis
$V^T$
The directional angles of the camera

Look at line 96 in tokens_utils.py:
return xc @ vt[:2].T
We throw away all rows of $V^T$ except the first 2 (vt[:2])! That single matrix multiplication drops 766 dimensions and gives us our $(x, y)$ coordinates!
